How to read a bird song spectrogram (sonogram)

A spectrogram, also called a sonogram, is a picture of sound. Once you know how to read one, a bird's song stops being a blur of notes and becomes a shape you can study. Here is what the picture shows and what to look for.

What a spectrogram shows

A spectrogram has three ingredients:

  • Left to right is time. The sound starts at the left and moves right, like a line of text.
  • Bottom to top is pitch. Low sounds sit near the bottom and high sounds near the top. Pitch is measured in hertz, or kilohertz (kHz) for high sounds.
  • Brightness or color is loudness. The louder a sound is at that moment and pitch, the brighter or stronger the mark.

Birdsong tends to sit in the middle and upper part. Many songbirds sing roughly between 2 and 8 kHz, while rumble from wind and traffic sits at the very bottom.

The names: spectrogram and sonogram

The two words mean much the same thing for this purpose. Researchers usually say spectrogram, and birders often say sonogram. If you search for "sonogram" alone you may get pregnancy ultrasounds, so add "bird" to your search.

Shapes and what they mean

What you seeWhat you hear
A flat, thin horizontal lineA steady, pure whistle
A line that curves up or downA slurred whistle that rises or falls
Short vertical marksClicks, taps or sharp notes
A row of evenly spaced marksA trill
A thick, fuzzy bandA buzz or a rasp
Several lines stacked above each otherA rich tone with overtones (harmonics)
A smudge near the bottomWind, traffic or handling noise

These are general patterns. The exact shape depends on the species and on how far away the bird is.

How to study one

  1. Find the phrases. A song is made of phrases with gaps between them. Spot where one ends and the next begins.
  2. Look at the length. Is it a short burst or a long, flowing song?
  3. Count repeats. Does the same phrase come back? Birds like the Carolina Wren repeat a phrase several times, while others sing a long, varied string.
  4. Check the pitch range. Is the song low, high or does it sweep through both?
  5. Compare with a reference. Play a known recording next to your own and see whether the shapes line up.

Why it helps

Sonograms help in three ways. They make a fast song slow enough to see. They show you what a sound is made of, so you can tell a trill from a buzz even when your ears are unsure. And they let you separate overlapping birds, since two singers at different pitches draw on different parts of the picture.

Limits

A spectrogram is a tool, not an answer. Distance, wind and echoes change how a song looks, and some species look alike. Treat the picture as a way to listen more carefully, and confirm an identification by sight or a second recording when you can.
