How to start a bird life list (and a year list)

A life list is a record of every bird species you have personally identified. It is simple to begin, it gives each outing a small goal, and it grows with you for years. Here is how to start one, and a year list alongside it.

What the lists are

  • Life list: every species you have identified, ever, anywhere.
  • Year list: the species you have identified in one calendar year, starting again on 1 January.
  • Local lists: some people also keep a garden or yard list, a "patch" list for a favorite park, or a county list.

Each one is just a list of species names with a few details. They can live in a notebook, a spreadsheet or an app.

Decide your own rules

It is your list, so you set the rules. A few things to decide:

  • Seen, heard or both? Many birders count a bird they identified only by sound, since often that is the only way to know it is there. Others keep a separate "seen" list. Pick one and stay with it.
  • Wild birds only. Most people count wild, free-flying birds, and leave out captive ones and obvious escapes.
  • How sure is sure? Add a bird only when you are confident. A doubtful bird can wait for a better look or listen.

If you want to compare your list with others, many birders follow published guidelines, such as those of the American Birding Association or their country's birding body. Check which rules apply where you live.

What to write down

For every new bird, record:

  • the species
  • the date
  • the place
  • how you identified it: seen, heard, or both
  • a short note on what made you sure

The note is the part people skip and later wish they had written. "Heard a loud rolling song from the hedge, then saw it on the fence" is far more useful than just the name.

Mind the taxonomy

The number of species depends on whose list you follow. Taxonomists sometimes split one species into two, or merge two into one, and different checklists do not always agree. Songlark uses the IOC World Bird List for names. Whatever you use, stay with one list so your totals stay comparable.

Ways to grow it

  • Start with the birds in your garden or local park, then widen the circle.
  • Go out at dawn, when birds are most vocal.
  • Visit different habitats such as woodland, wetland and coast.
  • Travel. A new country can add dozens of birds in a week.
  • Learn songs and calls. Many species are heard long before they are seen.

Free online tools such as eBird can also keep checklists and show what is around you.

Doing it with Songlark

Songlark has a Life List tab built in. It keeps the date, place and song of each bird you add, with counts for this year and for life.

  1. Listen with Start listening on the Listen tab and let a session finish.
  2. In the session summary, find Add to your life list. Add a bird only when you are confident. Songlark's own advice is that a Strong match is a good start, but seeing the bird is better.
  3. Add a place if you like, using the optional Place field, then tap Save.
  4. Already saved a recording? Open it from the Recordings tab and choose Add to life list for a species.
  5. Open the Life List tab and switch between Life and This year to see both lists. Use Search your list to find a bird.
  6. A bookmark marks birds that are already on your list, so you can tell at a glance which are new.

Exporting your life list as a CSV file for Numbers or Excel is part of Songlark Pro. Species names are available in 12 languages from the IOC World Bird List. See the help page for details on exporting.
