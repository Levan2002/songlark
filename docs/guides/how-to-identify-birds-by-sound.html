How to identify birds by sound: a beginner's guide

You hear a bird long before you see it, and often you never see it at all. Learning to identify birds by sound opens up a lot more of the birds around you. Here is a practical way to start, with or without an app.

Songs and calls are different

Birds make two broad kinds of sound, and it helps to know which one you are hearing.

  • Songs are longer and more musical. In many species the male sings them to claim a territory and attract a mate, mostly in the breeding season.
  • Calls are short and simple. They keep a flock together, signal alarm or say "I'm here". Many birds call all year.

Some birds are easier to learn by song, and some, such as many woodpeckers and gulls, by call. In winter you will mostly hear calls.

What to listen for

You do not need musical training. Ask a few simple questions about the sound:

  • Pitch. High and thin, or low and deep?
  • Tone. Is it a clear whistle, a buzz, a trill, a rasp or a chatter?
  • Rhythm and speed. Steady, slow, accelerating, or in bursts?
  • Pattern. Does the bird repeat one phrase over and over, or sing a long varied string?
  • Direction of the notes. Do they rise, fall or stay level?

Say it out loud or write it down in your own words. "Three slow whistles then a fast trill" is already a useful description.

Use memory tricks

People have been turning bird sounds into words for centuries. Some well-known ones:

  • Barred Owl: "who cooks for you, who cooks for you all"
  • Eastern Towhee: "drink your tea"
  • Carolina Wren: a loud, rolling "teakettle, teakettle, teakettle"
  • Great Tit: a squeaky "teacher, teacher"
  • Yellowhammer: "a little bit of bread and no cheese"

Make up your own if the traditional ones do not click. What matters is that the phrase fits the rhythm.

Narrow it down with clues

Sound is only one clue. Add the others:

  • Where you are. Region and time of year rule out most of the world's birds.
  • Habitat. Woodland, marsh, garden and open field each have their own voices.
  • Time of day. Singing is usually strongest around dawn, in the hour or so around sunrise.
  • Height. Is it from the canopy, the bushes or the ground?

If you can, look for the singer. Seeing the bird that made a sound is the quickest way to learn it for good.

A simple way to start

  1. Pick a handful of common birds near you and learn those first.
  2. Listen in the same place at the same time for a few mornings. You will start to hear repeats.
  3. Learn one sound at a time, and compare it with a recording.
  4. Keep a note of what you hear, with the date and place.
  5. Confirm by sight or by hearing it again before you count a bird as sure.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to learn everything at once. Start with the few birds around you.
  • Forgetting mimics. Mockingbirds, starlings and jays can copy other species. A rapid string of very different phrases from one bird is a clue.
  • Ignoring background noise. Wind, traffic and running water can hide or mimic bird sounds.
  • Trusting one listen. A distant, faint sound is easy to get wrong. Wait for it to repeat.

Doing it with Songlark

Songlark listens through your iPhone's microphone and suggests who is singing, right on the device, with no signal needed. It helps you identify birds by sound, and like any listener it can be wrong.

  1. Open Songlark on the Listen tab and tap Start listening. The first time, iPhone asks to use the microphone. Songlark needs it to hear birds.
  2. Hold your iPhone still with the bottom edge, where the microphone is, pointing toward the song.
  3. Watch the live sonogram. When a bird stands out, its name is pinned to the phrase it sang, usually within 5 to 10 seconds.
  4. Check the strength of each match: Strong match, Likely or Possible. A Possible match is a hint of what to look for, not an answer.
  5. If a result is marked Unusual here, look at your region. In Settings, Where you listen sets the region Songlark uses. It does not use your location.
  6. Keep listening. A bird that sings several times gives Songlark more to go on than a single call.
  7. Tap Save to keep the session, or add birds you are sure of to your life list.
